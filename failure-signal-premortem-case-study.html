Catching the Failure Signal Early

A pre-mortem case study for a multi-city diagnostics rollout

Entry depth: −4,000 m (rollout plan)
Surfaced at: Abyssal zone
Pre-MortemStrategic ManagementDiagnostics
Finding

The plan was costed to the last site, but it failed on sequencing. Sites would open faster than referrals and trained staff could be secured.

Situation

A diagnostics operator had a fully costed plan to open lab collection points across several cities within a single quarter. The budget, the sites and the timetable were all in place. What the plan did not test was whether the things a site depends on would arrive in the same order as the sites.

Anonymised engagement. Client details are withheld and the case is described at the level of method and decision, not company data. The point is the way the plan was stress-tested, which applies to any multi-site rollout.

The descent

Pre-Mortem starts from an assumption that sounds odd at first: the launch has already failed. The team then works backwards to the most likely reason. Here the plan was run forward, quarter by quarter, until it reached its failure point.

Three depths, one question

−4,000 m · ROLLOUT PLAN

A fully costed plan: collection points opening across several cities within a single quarter.

MID-DESCENT · PLAN RUN FORWARD

Each dependency tracked on its own clock: site opening, referral partnerships, trained staff.

ABYSSAL ZONE · SURFACED

Sites open faster than referrals and staff can be secured, leaving capacity underused in month two or three.

What the pre-mortem asked of each dependency

A site is not a revenue line until three other things exist around it. The pre-mortem gave each one its own timeline and compared them with the opening schedule.

DependencyQuestion askedWhat it showed
Site opening scheduleHow quickly can collection points physically open across the cities in the quarter?Openings were the fastest-moving part of the plan and set the pace for everything else.
Referral partnershipsWhen would doctors and clinics near each site actually start sending patients, and what has to happen first?Referral relationships take longer to build than a site takes to open, so volume would trail the doors.
Trained staffCould each site be staffed with trained collection personnel on its opening day?Staffing was planned in parallel with openings, not ahead of them, so some sites would open short.
Capacity utilisationWhat happens to the cost base if sites are open but volume and staffing lag?Underused capacity in month two or three, with fixed costs running against volume that had not arrived.

Findings are stated qualitatively because the engagement is anonymised. In a live engagement each row carries its own dates and figures.

Two ways to roll out

The original plan treated the quarter as the unit of delivery. The pre-mortem suggested treating readiness as the unit instead.

Open on the calendar
Open on readiness
Approach
All cities in one quarter

Sites open as fast as they can be fitted out. Referrals and staff are chased afterwards.

Phased, tier-based

Cities are tiered, and each tier opens only when its referral pipeline and staffing are locked in.

Likely result
Doors open, volume trails

Idle capacity in the first months, with the cost of the full network carried early.

Volume meets capacity

Each site opens into demand that has been arranged, so utilisation starts closer to plan.

Surfaced with

A phased, tier-based rollout in which referral pipelines and staffing were locked in before each site opened rather than after, avoiding the exact failure mode the pre-mortem had surfaced.

  1. TIER 1

    Open the first cities where referral and staffing readiness is highest. Use them to learn what a ready site looks like.

  2. TIER 2

    Release the next cities only when their referral pipelines and trained staff are confirmed, using what Tier 1 taught about timing.

  3. TIER 3

    Complete the network on the same gate: a site opens when its readiness is in place, not when the calendar says so.

What a rollout team should take from it

  1. Run the plan forward and ask which dependency will lag first. In a multi-site rollout it is rarely the building.
  2. Give referrals, staffing and opening dates separate clocks, then compare them. The gap between the clocks is the risk.
  3. Make readiness a condition of opening each site, set out in advance and applied the same way to every tier.
  4. Watch utilisation in the first months as the early signal, and agree beforehand what level triggers a pause.
  5. Treat a fully costed plan as a starting point. A cost model shows what the rollout costs, not whether its order works.
Scope. Commercial and operational analysis based on the dependencies described. Confirm licensing, referral and clinical-quality requirements with qualified advisers in each market.
