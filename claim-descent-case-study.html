The Claim That Couldn't Survive the Descent

A national pharma launch, three headline claims, and what happened when each was graded before it reached the media.

Entry depth0 m — surface claim
Surfaced atTwilight zone
Semantic BIPre-MortemBrand Differentiation

Finding

Two of the three headline claims could not be proven wrong, so they could not be defended either. The positioning beneath them was language half the category already used. The campaign was rebuilt on the one differentiator the product could verify.

Situation

A regional pharmaceutical manufacturer had built a national launch campaign around three headline claims about efficacy and differentiation. The materials were due to go to media within weeks. Internal enthusiasm was high, and the claims had been repeated in enough meetings that they had started to feel like facts.

The brief to AIMMS was narrow: read the claims as a competitor, a journalist or a regulator would read them, and say which ones would hold.

Client
Regional pharmaceutical manufacturer, anonymised.
Decision at stake
Whether to take three headline claims into a national media launch.
Time available
A few weeks before media placement.
Lenses used
Semantic BI Analyst, Pre-Mortem Foresighted Intelligence Analyst, Strategic Brand Differentiation Specialist.

The descent

The engine starts every engagement at the surface and works down. Each pass adds pressure and removes what does not hold. Here the claims entered at 0 m and were read through three lenses.

First passSemantic BI

Grade each claim on the surface

Every headline claim was graded as Verified, Plausible-Unverified or Affect-Driven / Unfalsifiable. The test is simple: what evidence, if it appeared, would show the sentence to be wrong? Where no such evidence can be named, the claim carries feeling rather than information.

Two of three: Affect-Driven / Unfalsifiable
Second passPre-Mortem

Run the launch forward to the challenge

The pre-mortem assumed the campaign had already been publicly challenged and asked what a competitor or a journalist would say first. It mapped the questions in the order they would be asked, from "what is this based on?" to "why is this different from the other brands?"

The two ungraded claims drew the hardest questions first
Third passBrand Differentiation

Test whether the positioning is actually different

The underlying positioning was generic "innovation-led" language. It could be printed unchanged by any competitor in the category, and half of them already did.

Legal to say, but it positioned nobody

The claims, graded

Headline claim 2Superiority framing No comparator, population or measure that a rival could check. A competitor can print the same sentence about its own product. Affect-Driven / Unfalsifiable

Grades are an AIMMS reading of the client's own launch materials, not a legal or regulatory finding.

Why the positioning failed the test

A claim can fail in two ways, and this campaign showed both. Two claims could not be tested. The positioning underneath them could be tested and was simply shared.

Surfaced with

A narrower claim set the product could stand behind

The campaign went forward on the one verifiable differentiator the product actually had. The wording was specific enough to survive both legal review and public scrutiny.

Before the descent

  • Three headline claims, two with no way to be proven wrong.
  • Positioning any competitor could copy line for line.
  • No prepared answer to the first public challenge.

After the descent

  • A smaller set of claims, each tied to something checkable.
  • Language built on the product's one verifiable difference.
  • Each sentence able to answer "what is this based on?"

What to take from it

  1. Fewer claims make a stronger campaign. A claim you cannot defend weakens the ones you can.
  2. Ask what would prove each sentence wrong. If nothing could, rewrite it or retire it.
  3. Do not replace a risky claim with a vaguer one. Vague language is safer, but it is also empty.
  4. Build the headline on the differentiator you can verify. Then check that no competitor can print the same sentence.
  5. Run the pre-mortem before media, not after. Find the first question a journalist would ask while the answer is still cheap to fix.
Scope. Commercial analysis, not legal advice. Final claim wording should be cleared with qualified regulatory and legal counsel in each market.
