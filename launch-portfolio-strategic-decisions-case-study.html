The Six-Launch Autopsy

A strategic-decisions case study for a stalled pharma launch portfolio

Portfolio StrategyPre-Mortem
Finding

A mid-size Pakistani pharma company launched six brands in 2025. By the November survey, none had earned its place. The question was not how to push harder but which brands deserve the next rupee, and which decisions come first.

CategoryPortfolio Strategy, Post-Launch Review
MarketPakistan, branded generics
Entry depth0 m, the launch itself
Surfaced atTwilight zone: a sequenced, gated plan

The Situation

Six brands entered the market in 2025: a beta-blocker (nebivolol), a prokinetic (levosulpiride), a carbapenem (meropenem), a neuro-pain brand (pregabalin), an ossein-mineral bone supplement with vitamin D3, and a metformin-based SGLT2/DPP-4 diabetes combination. The November 2025 market survey showed all six below expectation.

The wider market explains part of it. As quoted in the source brief, national pharma value grew about 20.6% in 2025 while unit growth was about 3.6%. Growth came from price increases, not from more patients treated. A new brand cannot ride that tide. It has to take units from an incumbent, one prescriber at a time.

Market size and growth figures are quoted from the source brief and were not independently verified. Treat them as scenario inputs.

The six problems are not the same problem. Treating them with one "clinical advocacy" programme and one 15% target hides that. The first strategic decision was to separate them.

Precedent: When Success Invites Copies

The company's own history carries a warning. In the 2005 to 2007 period, one high-value brand grew from roughly 9,000 to 40,000 packs a month. By 2007, more than 14 me-too brands from other companies had been registered in Pakistan. In this market, visible success is the signal that triggers copying. A brand that proves a niche without a defence will be crowded out within two years.

That lesson shapes every decision below: build position where copying is slow (institutions, indication-specific evidence, prescriber relationships), not where it is quick (price, packaging, generic claims).

The Strategic Decisions

Decision 1: Sequence the portfolio, do not blitz it

Choice. Fund two lead brands, repair two, defend one, and hold one.

Why. A single field force cannot deliver six new positionings to the same 500 consultants in one quarter. Call frequency per brand falls, and every message is forgotten.

Trade-off. Four brands wait. Their short-term numbers may slip further.

Gate. Review at day 90. A brand moves up a tier only on prescription evidence, not on field activity.

Decision 2: Lead with the diabetes combination in a narrow patient segment

Choice. Position it for patients already failing dual therapy and at high cardiovascular or renal risk, not for all type 2 diabetes.

Why. The strongest clinical story sits where the premium is easiest to justify. A broad price-led push would meet cheaper generics head on.

Trade-off. A smaller patient pool, accepted for better margin and a clearer message.

Gate. Repeat-prescription rate among targeted diabetologists at 60 days.

Decision 3: Take meropenem to institutions, with a stewardship stance

Choice. Drop retail promotion. Work through hospital pharmacy committees with batch quality and stability documentation.

Why. The buying decision sits with committees, not individual prescribers. Documented quality is the one lever a cheaper unbranded generic cannot copy quickly.

Trade-off. Slow cycles. Formulary wins take quarters, not weeks.

Gate. Number of formulary submissions accepted, tracked per institution.

Decision 4: Hold pregabalin promotion and reduce regulatory exposure

Choice. Limit it to documented neuropathic indications, with no volume targets on the brand.

Why. Misuse concerns make aggressive promotion a regulatory and reputational liability. A volume incentive on this brand is the wrong incentive.

Trade-off. Revenue forgone in exchange for licence safety.

Gate. Compliance review each quarter. Reopen only if the regulatory picture clears.

Decision 5: Repair the bone brand through gynaecology, and the prokinetic through evidence

Choice. Concentrate the bone brand on gynaecologists and post-menopausal patients. For the prokinetic, build a small local case series before any "brain-gut" claim goes into the field.

Why. Both brands depend on claims their incumbents can answer easily. Local evidence is slower to copy than a tagline.

Trade-off. Delay of one to two quarters while evidence is gathered.

Gate. Claim sign-off by medical affairs before field use.

Decision 6: Defend the beta-blocker, do not expand it

Choice. Keep existing prescribers served. Make no new investment until the leads show results.

Why. A crowded class rewards incumbents. Spending here starves the two brands with a real chance.

Trade-off. The brand may plateau.

Gate. Revisit at day 90 with the lead brands' results in hand.

The Autopsy: What the First Plan Got Wrong

The original recovery plan was energetic and complete. It was also uniform. The findings:

  1. One target for six problems. A flat 15% unit-share goal ignores that a restricted brand, an institutional brand and a premium brand have different ceilings.
  2. Projections presented as evidence. The recovery chart showed target indices of 115 to 125 against a baseline of 100. These were aspirations, not forecasts. Shown to a managing director as a trajectory, they set up a miss.
  3. Claims ahead of proof. Statements such as "faster onset", "proven stability", and "significantly more effective than calcium carbonate" need local or published support before a representative says them to a consultant.
  4. Six simultaneous pivots. Retraining, a consultant blitz, a pharmacy loyalty scheme, free diagnostic services and a digital portal in one quarter exceeds execution capacity.
  5. Safety treated as marketing. Pregabalin was framed as a growth opportunity when its main task is risk containment. A comparable lesson sits in the history of domperidone, where cardiac-safety concerns led to restricted use.
Product composition for the diabetes combination was inconsistent in the source material (described both generally as SGLT2/DPP-4 and as a specific triple). Confirm the registered formulation before any claim is made.

Discussion Questions

  1. Which two brands would you fund first, and what evidence would change your mind?
  2. Is a brand with regulatory risk worth any promotional spend, and under what conditions?
  3. How should a company set targets when the market grows by price, not volume?
  4. What makes a position hard to copy in a market where 14 look-alikes can appear in a year?
Outcome: A portfolio decision that fits the field force's real capacity: two lead brands funded, two repaired, one defended and one held, with a day-90 gate where every brand has to earn its next tier of investment through prescription evidence rather than activity.
