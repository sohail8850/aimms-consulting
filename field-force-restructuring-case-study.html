The Field Force Expansion Autopsy

What a documented restructuring teaches about readiness and sequencing

Strategic Management Pre-Mortem
The lesson

A restructure is only as sound as the managers, tools and incentives that carry it, and all three must be ready before the first territory moves.

The brief

The Field Force Restructuring Advisory project rests on one claim: the order of a national reorganisation matters as much as its design. This case study tests that claim against a documented public example.

The example is an anonymised US pharmaceutical manufacturer described by a sales-effectiveness practice leader in Pharmaceutical Executive. Its design intent was reasonable and its market was not the problem. What went wrong was readiness: the people, tools and incentives the new structure depended on were not in place when it went live.

The Strategic Management Advisor and Pre-Mortem Foresighted Intelligence Analyst lenses of the AIMMS Strategic Intelligence Engine were applied to the published record, asking which warning signs were visible and which gate would have caught each one.

How this reference is used. The facts in the timeline come from one published article (listed under Sources). The company is not named there, and AIMMS Consulting was not engaged by it. The article's own diagnosis centres on first-line manager capability. The mapping to warning signals and gates in this page is AIMMS Consulting's reading of that record, not a claim made by the article.

The benchmark: a field force that outran its managers

A mid-sized US manufacturer launched a newly acquired CNS product through a field force that was also promoting a basket of "me-too" primary-care drugs.

  1. Before launch

    The launch added highly specialised customers to each representative's target list. The company changed its selling approach and expanded the field force by 50 percent. About a dozen new first-line manager positions were created, most filled by promoting its highest-performing representatives.

  2. At launch

    Decisions on how to allocate resources locally, previously taken centrally at headquarters, were devolved to the new first-line managers, who were expected to analyse their own districts and decide.

  3. One year in

    Sales were clearly set to fall significantly short of expectations. In many parts of the country representatives received no incentive payout or only a small one. Field motivation was low and many representatives chose to leave.

  4. What the field showed

    Many managers and teams reverted to their old selling behaviours. Managers lacking experience and tools allocated resources by trial and error, which caused inconsistency across the company. A few teams adapted and succeeded, but there was no process to learn from them.

  5. The response

    The company adopted a "more realistic" forecast and cut the field force in half. Repeated restructuring disrupted the rest of the portfolio, and further cuts were possible when the article was written.

The article states that this was not a product or market problem. A smaller European specialty pharma company in the same piece took a different path: it involved its managers in the analysis from the start and gave them a coaching process, tools and a dashboard before expecting new behaviour in the field.

The Pre-Mortem reading

Each fact in the record is matched to the early-warning signal it would have produced and the gate that would have stopped it.

What the record shows Warning signal it would have raised Gate that answers it
Top representatives promoted into a dozen manager roles Manager readiness: appointed, not yet capable in the new role. Wave 0 gate: managers named and trained for the new role, with selection tested on managerial strength, not past sales alone.
Allocation authority devolved to managers who lacked experience and tools Data mismatch: decisions made without a common rulebook or reliable district data. Authority is devolved only after allocation tools, data and a decision rulebook pass a dry run.
Teams reverting to old selling behaviours Call-rate and behaviour drop in the first weeks after go-live. Wave 1 pilot measures behaviour as well as sales, and the playbook is fixed before Wave 2 opens.
Small or zero incentive payouts Resignation pattern: motivation falls where pay is detached from the new job. Parallel incentive bridge, so no one is penalised for the restructure itself.
Representatives leaving in many regions Resignation pattern clustered by region or manager. Named retention list for critical talent and a weekly attrition trigger with an owner.
A few successful teams, no process to learn from them Information lag: good practice invisible to the centre. Pilot-wave fix log: what worked and what failed is written into the playbook before the next wave.
Field force cut in half, then repeated restructuring Repeat disruption: each correction touches customers who were already disturbed. One controlled sequence with a Stabilise gate, instead of successive cuts after the fact.

The first column reflects the published record. The second and third columns are AIMMS Consulting's reading, using the Pre-Mortem Warning Constellation and the wave gates from the Field Force Restructuring Advisory project.

The readiness test

Before the first territory moves, a national restructure should be able to answer all three questions with evidence.

Is every manager ready?

Named, selected for the new role, and trained. A manager who was a strong representative is not automatically a strong manager of the new model.

Does every decision have its tools?

Any authority moved to the field comes with data, a rulebook and a dashboard. Trial and error at district level becomes inconsistency at national level.

Will the first signals reach the centre?

Call rates, orders, attrition and handover gaps visible weekly, each with an owner and a trigger, so a problem is found in week two and not at the year-end review.

The wave-opening rule

A wave opens only when [managers named and trained], [tools and data proven on a dry run] and [warning signals owned and tracked] for every territory in it. If any is missing, the wave waits.

What this means for a national restructure

  • Design is necessary but not sufficient: the same design can succeed or fail on readiness alone
  • Promotion into manager roles is a selection decision and needs its own criteria
  • Devolved authority needs tools and rules first
  • Incentives must follow the new structure from day one, not trail it
  • A pilot wave is cheaper than a half-sized field force
  • Every correction after go-live disturbs customers a second time

These points are why the Field Force Restructuring Advisory builds readiness gates into every wave, and why the exposed territories move last.

Scope note. This is a commercial analysis of one published example and does not describe any AIMMS client. Employment, HR and legal steps in a restructure should be cleared with qualified counsel for each jurisdiction.
